1. What DiagMC samples

Diagrammatic Monte Carlo (DiagMC) evaluates sums and integrals in a Feynman diagram expansion by stochastic sampling. It samples diagram order, connectivity, internal momenta and imaginary times to calculate Green's functions, self-energies and other observables. [1]

Suppose the target quantity can be written as

Q=∑n=0∞∑Tn∫dXn W(n,Tn,Xn).Q=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\mathcal T_n} \int dX_n\,W(n,\mathcal T_n,X_n).Q=n=0∑∞​Tn​∑​∫dXn​W(n,Tn​,Xn​).

Here nnn is the expansion order, Tn\mathcal T_nTn​ is the diagram topology, and XnX_nXn​ contains the internal integration variables. A Monte Carlo configuration is a particular diagram together with specific values for all its variables. The algorithm moves between configurations so that important contributions are visited more frequently.

When weights have either sign, one usually samples ∣W∣|W|∣W∣ and includes the sign in the measurements. Including higher orders can go beyond low-order perturbation theory, but correct sampling and convergence of the series must be checked separately. Stochastic sampling does not itself resolve convergence problems in the series. [2]

2. The Holstein model and propagators

Set ℏ=a=1\hbar=a=1ℏ=a=1 and consider a ddd-dimensional hypercubic lattice with nearest-neighbor hopping:

H=−t∑⟨ij⟩(ci†cj+h.c.)+ω0∑ibi†bi+g∑ini(bi†+bi).\begin{aligned} H={}&-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle} (c_i^\dagger c_j+\mathrm{h.c.})\\ &+\omega_0\sum_i b_i^\dagger b_i +g\sum_i n_i(b_i^\dagger+b_i). \end{aligned}H=​−t⟨ij⟩∑​(ci†​cj​+h.c.)+ω0​i∑​bi†​bi​+gi∑​ni​(bi†​+bi​).​

ci†c_i^\daggerci†​ and bi†b_i^\daggerbi†​ create an electron and a local phonon, respectively, and ni=ci†cin_i=c_i^\dagger c_ini​=ci†​ci​. ttt is the electron hopping amplitude, ω0>0\omega_0>0ω0​>0 is the dispersionless phonon energy, and ggg is a coupling constant with units of energy. The spin index can be omitted in the single-electron problem.

The free-electron dispersion is

εk=−2t∑α=1dcos⁡kα.\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=-2t\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\cos k_\alpha.εk​=−2tα=1∑d​coskα​.

The Holstein coupling is local in real space, so its momentum-space vertex is independent of phonon momentum. [3]

The most direct quantity to calculate is the single-electron imaginary-time propagator:

G(k,τ)=⟨vac∣ cke−τ(H−μNe)ck†∣vac⟩,τ>0,Ne=∑ini.\begin{aligned} \mathcal G(\mathbf k,\tau) ={}&\langle\mathrm{vac}|\,c_{\mathbf k} e^{-\tau(H-\mu N_e)} c_{\mathbf k}^\dagger|\mathrm{vac}\rangle,\\ &\tau>0,\qquad N_e=\sum_i n_i. \end{aligned}G(k,τ)=​⟨vac∣ck​e−τ(H−μNe​)ck†​∣vac⟩,τ>0,Ne​=i∑​ni​.​

∣vac⟩|\mathrm{vac}\rangle∣vac⟩ contains neither electrons nor phonons. Our definition of G\mathcal GG omits the overall minus sign of the conventional fermionic Green's function. The auxiliary parameter μ\muμ can be chosen below the lowest single-electron energy so that the propagator decays in imaginary time; here it is used to control sampling.

Physically, a bare electron with momentum k\mathbf kk is added to the vacuum, interacts with phonons for an imaginary-time interval, and is then projected back onto the bare-electron state.

4. Second-order diagrams: sequential, nested and crossing

For four ordered vertices τ1<τ2<τ3<τ4\tau_1\lt\tau_2\lt\tau_3\lt\tau_4τ1​<τ2​<τ3​<τ4​, there are three phonon pairings at n=2n=2n=2. All endpoints lie on the same electron line.

A full DiagMC expansion must include crossing diagrams. Excluding a class of diagrams gives an approximation restricted to the remaining classes. Allowing inserted phonon endpoints to span existing vertices is one way to access these topologies.

5. Monte Carlo updates and normalization

The sampling scheme is built around W(C)W(C)W(C). Conceptually, it should allow the following changes:

  • Insert or remove a phonon line to change the diagram order.
  • Move vertices in imaginary time and change phonon momenta.
  • Visit different pairings, including crossings; additional reconnection updates can improve sampling efficiency.
  • When sampling an interval of G(τ)\mathcal G(\tau)G(τ), also update the external imaginary time.

For insertion, removal and displacement updates using these coordinates directly, the acceptance probability has the Metropolis–Hastings form:

Pacc(C→C′)=min⁡ ⁣[1,W(C′)T(C′→C)W(C)T(C→C′)].P_{\mathrm{acc}}(C\to C') =\min\!\left[ 1,\frac{W(C')T(C'\to C)}{W(C)T(C\to C')} \right].Pacc​(C→C′)=min[1,W(C)T(C→C′)W(C′)T(C′→C)​].

TTT includes the update type, line selection, and proposal densities for new times and momenta. A coordinate transformation with a nontrivial Jacobian requires the corresponding factor. The design must satisfy detailed balance and reach all configurations that contribute.

(5) From two worked updates to actual sampling

Repeat: choose an update → propose internal variables → evaluate old and new WWW and TTT → draw a number to accept or reject. After warmup, record the current configuration at every measurement step, including repeats after rejection. Recording only accepted updates changes the visit frequencies.

When sampling directly according to WWW without extra bias, the visit frequencies already follow WWW. Each measurement counts one visit; visits must not receive another factor of WWW. Arbitrary endpoint insertions and line removals can reach every finite-order pairing via order zero, including crossings. Time and momentum moves can improve efficiency. Finally, use the zero-order visit frequency below to obtain the absolute normalization of G(k,τ)\mathcal G(k,\tau)G(k,τ).

5.2 Normalization from zero-order visits

The known zero-order contribution therefore provides a reference:

G(k,τ)=G0(k,τ)Pr⁡(n=0).\mathcal G(\mathbf k,\tau) =\frac{\mathcal G_0(\mathbf k,\tau)}{\Pr(n=0)}.G(k,τ)=Pr(n=0)G0​(k,τ)​.

A separate reference sector can also be introduced. If sampling is biased or external variables are sampled jointly, the normalization must be adjusted; the fixed-variable formula above cannot be applied unchanged. [1]

6. Extracting observables from the propagator

The propagator contains information about the single-electron spectrum. If an isolated lowest-energy polaron state exists at the chosen momentum and has nonzero overlap with the bare-electron state, its long-imaginary-time behavior is

G(k,τ)∼Zke−(Ek−μ)τ.\mathcal G(\mathbf k,\tau) \sim Z_{\mathbf k}e^{-(E_{\mathbf k}-\mu)\tau}.G(k,τ)∼Zk​e−(Ek​−μ)τ.

The logarithmic slope gives the energy:

Ek=μ−lim⁡τ→∞∂τln⁡G(k,τ).E_{\mathbf k}=\mu-\lim_{\tau\to\infty} \partial_\tau\ln\mathcal G(\mathbf k,\tau).Ek​=μ−τ→∞lim​∂τ​lnG(k,τ).

After absolute normalization, the exponential prefactor gives the quasiparticle residue:

Zk=lim⁡τ→∞e(Ek−μ)τG(k,τ).Z_{\mathbf k}=\lim_{\tau\to\infty} e^{(E_{\mathbf k}-\mu)\tau}\mathcal G(\mathbf k,\tau).Zk​=τ→∞lim​e(Ek​−μ)τG(k,τ).

For an isotropic system, the small-momentum dispersion near its minimum gives the effective mass:

Ek=E0+∣k∣22m∗+⋯ .E_{\mathbf k}=E_{\mathbf 0} +\frac{|\mathbf k|^2}{2m^*}+\cdots.Ek​=E0​+2m∗∣k∣2​+⋯.

This is the standard polaron DiagMC approach. [5] If the lowest spectral edge is a continuum rather than an isolated pole, a single exponential with a nonzero constant ZkZ_{\mathbf k}Zk​ cannot simply be assumed to describe the full asymptotic behavior.

Obtaining the full spectral function A(k,ω)A(\mathbf k,\omega)A(k,ω) requires analytic continuation from imaginary time to real frequency. Ground-state energies and effective masses can instead be extracted directly from the long-time behavior above.

  3. Low-order comparison. The one-phonon contribution should agree with direct integration, testing the integration measure, momentum conservation and combinatorial counting.
  4. Order and long-time convergence. Results should remain stable within errors when increasing the allowed order and moving the long-time fitting window. Stable statistics at a single fixed cutoff do not establish convergence.
  5. Statistical reliability. Error estimates must account for autocorrelation. At strong coupling, also verify that order zero or the reference sector is visited sufficiently often.

Nonnegative single-polaron weights do not guarantee inexpensive calculations. Strong coupling, low phonon frequencies and longer imaginary times typically shift important contributions to higher diagram orders.

Record error sources separately: Monte Carlo statistics, order truncation, excited-state contamination at finite imaginary time, and any momentum or time discretization. Continuous momentum integration can address the infinite lattice directly, but an implementation using a discrete grid still requires grid-convergence checks.

8. Finite electron density and Bold DiagMC

At finite electron density, propagators include occupation effects and diagrams can contain closed electron loops. At finite temperature, phonon propagation also includes thermal absorption. The nonnegativity of the single-polaron expansion does not carry over automatically. This concerns diagram-level signs; it does not imply that every Monte Carlo representation of the Holstein model has a sign problem.

Main differences between the two physical problems
AspectZero-temperature single polaronFinite electron density

Main observablesEkE_{\mathbf k}Ek​, ZkZ_{\mathbf k}Zk​, m∗m^*m∗Many-body self-energies, response functions and possible ordered states

One approach is Bold DiagMC: construct skeleton diagrams from dressed electron and phonon propagators, sample the electron self-energy and phonon polarization, and update the propagators self-consistently through Dyson equations. Exclude subdiagrams already included in the dressed lines to avoid double counting, and check order convergence independently.

Such studies exist for the Holstein model at finite density. [6] They demonstrate normal-state calculations in particular parameter regimes. Charge-density waves and superconductivity require suitable two-particle correlations and instability analyses; the single-polaron propagator above does not directly yield a many-body phase diagram.

9. Preparation before implementation

A useful starting point is a nearest-neighbor lattice, zero temperature and one electron, directly sampling the full G\mathcal GG expansion built from bare propagators.

Before writing a solver, work through three steps on paper:

  1. Enumerate the first two orders and associate every line with its weight factor.
  2. Derive detailed balance for a paired insertion/removal update and show that all pairings can be reached.
  3. Derive normalization and observable estimators, and test them in the free and atomic limits.

These derivations provide a sound baseline before considering sampling efficiency, self-energy expansions or Bold schemes.
